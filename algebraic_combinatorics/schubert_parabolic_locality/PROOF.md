# Proof of parabolic locality and the componentwise Grassmannian theorem

All simple transpositions are indexed from one:
\(s_i=(i,i+1)\).  Reduced words therefore have positive integer letters.

## 1. Coxeter-support factorization

For \(w\in S_n\), let \(\operatorname{supp}(w)\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n-1\}\)
be its Coxeter support, the set of simple generators occurring in a reduced
word.  This set is independent of the chosen reduced word.  Write its
connected components in the type-\(A\) Coxeter graph as disjoint integer
intervals

\[
I_1,\ldots,I_r.
\]

The corresponding standard parabolic subgroup is the direct product of the
groups generated by the \(I_j\).  Consequently there are unique commuting
factors \(w^{(j)}\), each embedded in \(S_n\) and supported on \(I_j\), such
that

\[
w=w^{(1)}\cdots w^{(r)},\qquad
\ell(w)=\sum_j\ell(w^{(j)}).
\]

Every reduced word of \(w\) is uniquely a shuffle of one reduced word from
each factor.  Put \(L_j=\ell(w^{(j)})\) and \(L=\sum_jL_j\).

Macdonald's identity is

\[
\Upsilon_v=\frac1{\ell(v)!}
\sum_{(a_1,\ldots,a_{\ell(v)})\in\operatorname{Red}(v)}
a_1\cdots a_{\ell(v)}. \tag{3}
\]

The product of the letters is unchanged by a shuffle.  There are
\(L!/(L_1!\cdots L_r!)\) shuffles for each tuple of component words.
Substitution in (3) gives

\[
\begin{aligned}
\Upsilon_w
&=\frac1{L!}\frac{L!}{\prod_jL_j!}
  \prod_j\left(
    \sum_{a\in\operatorname{Red}(w^{(j)})}\prod_{q=1}^{L_j}a_q
  \right)\\
&=\prod_j\Upsilon_{w^{(j)}}. \tag{4}
\end{aligned}
\]

It is important here that the factors remain embedded in \(S_n\): their
reduced-word letters retain their absolute indices.  Standardizing a factor
would change its principal specialization.

## 2. Inflation preserves the factorization

Identity-block inflation is the homomorphism

\[
\iota_k:S_n\longrightarrow S_{kn},\qquad
\iota_k(w)(k(i-1)+r)=k(w(i)-1)+r
\]

for \(1\leq r\leq k\).  Every inversion of \(w\) becomes exactly \(k^2\)
inversions, so

\[
\ell(\iota_k(w))=k^2\ell(w). \tag{5}
\]

Since \(\iota_k\) is a homomorphism,

\[
\iota_k(w)=\prod_j\iota_k(w^{(j)}),
\]

and (5) makes this product length-additive.  Components separated by a
missing simple generator act on disjoint consecutive point intervals.
Their inflated point intervals are still disjoint, with the simple generator
at the scaled boundary missing.  Hence the Coxeter supports of the
\(\iota_k(w^{(j)})\) are again disconnected.  Applying (4) in \(S_{kn}\)
gives

\[
\Upsilon_{\iota_k(w)}=
\prod_j\Upsilon_{\iota_k(w^{(j)})}. \tag{6}
\]

Dividing (6) by the \(k^2\)-th power of (4) proves

\[
R_k(w)=\prod_jR_k(w^{(j)}). \tag{7}
\]

This proves the locality theorem and immediately shows that any collection
of component classes satisfying the identity-inflation inequality is closed
under parabolic products.

## 3. The Grassmannian component inequality

For completeness, here is the all-\(k\) Grassmannian input needed for the
stated corollary.  Suppose \(v\) is Grassmannian with its only descent at
\(d\).  Pad its partition to length \(d\):

\[
\lambda=(\lambda_1\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_d\geq0).
\]

The Grassmannian Schubert-to-Schur identity and the Weyl dimension formula
give

\[
\Upsilon_v=s_\lambda(1^d)
=\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq d}
\frac{\lambda_i-\lambda_j+j-i}{j-i}. \tag{8}
\]

The inflated permutation \(\iota_k(v)\) is Grassmannian with descent
\(kd\) and padded partition

\[
\lambda^{[k]}=
(\underbrace{k\lambda_1,\ldots,k\lambda_1}_{k},\ldots,
 \underbrace{k\lambda_d,\ldots,k\lambda_d}_{k}). \tag{9}
\]

Fix \(i<j\).  In the Weyl product for (9), pair the residues
\(r,s\in\{1,\ldots,k\}\).  Set

\[
A=k(\lambda_i-\lambda_j+j-i),\qquad
B=k(j-i),\qquad x=s-r.
\]

The corresponding factor is \((A+x)/(B+x)\).  Since
\(A\geq B>|x|\), pairing \((r,s)\) with \((s,r)\) gives

\[
\frac{A+x}{B+x}\frac{A-x}{B-x}
=\frac{A^2-x^2}{B^2-x^2}
\geq\frac{A^2}{B^2}. \tag{10}
\]

Indeed, the difference between the two sides is

\[
\frac{x^2(A^2-B^2)}{B^2(B^2-x^2)}\geq0.
\]

The factors with \(r=s\) equal \(A/B\).  Multiplying (10) over all
\(k^2\) residue pairs and then over all \(i<j\) yields

\[
s_{\lambda^{[k]}}(1^{kd})
\geq s_\lambda(1^d)^{k^2}. \tag{11}
\]

The identity permutation is the empty-partition case.  Thus every
Grassmannian component satisfies the all-\(k\) inequality.  Combining
(11) componentwise with (7) proves the componentwise Grassmannian theorem.

If \(k>1\), the comparison for a pair \(i<j\) is strict whenever
\(\lambda_i>\lambda_j\), because some residue pair has \(x\ne0\).
This also gives the equality cases directly from the padded component
partitions.
