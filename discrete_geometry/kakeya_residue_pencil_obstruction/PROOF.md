# A residue-pencil obstruction for Kakeya minimizers over length-two rings

Let `R` be a finite commutative local ring with identity. Assume its
maximal ideal is nonzero and principal, `m=(pi)`, that `m^2=0`, and that
the residue field `F=R/m` has odd order `q`. Thus `|R|=q^2`.
Examples include `Z/p^2 Z`, `F_q[epsilon]/(epsilon^2)`, and unramified
length-two Galois rings of odd residue characteristic.

A direction is a rank-one free submodule generated by a **unimodular**
vector `(a,b)`, meaning that at least one coordinate is a unit. Two such
vectors give the same direction exactly when they differ by multiplication
by a unit. A full affine line is `c+Rv`. A Kakeya set contains a line in
every direction. A witnessing line system chooses one such line in every
direction; it need not contain every line present in the set.

Call a witnessing system a **residue pencil** if all its reduced affine
lines in `F^2` contain the same point. This does not require the original
lines to have a common point in `R^2`.

**Theorem.** Every Kakeya set `K` admitting a residue-pencil witnessing
system satisfies

\[
 |K|\ \ge\ L_q:=\frac{q^4+q^3+q-1}{2}.                 \tag{1}
\]

There is an explicit Kakeya set `K_*` in the same `R^2` with

\[
 |K_*|=U_q:=\frac{q^4+q^2}{2}.                          \tag{2}
\]

Consequently no cardinality-minimizing Kakeya set admits any residue-pencil
witnessing system. More quantitatively, every such system costs at least

\[
 L_q-U_q=\frac{(q-1)(q^2+1)}2>0                         \tag{3}
\]

points above the explicit competitor. We do **not** assert that either
bound is the unrestricted minimum, or that (1) is attained.

This is an obstruction for a complete class of first-residue configurations.
It is a finite-ring analogue in the Kakeya research program, with no claimed
consequence for the Euclidean Hausdorff-dimension conjecture. The argument
uses the established odd-order finite-field Kakeya minimum; that input is
not a new result here.

## 1. Ring facts and the finite-field input

Multiplication by `pi` induces an isomorphism of additive groups, and of
`F`-modules,

\[
 F\longrightarrow\mathfrak m,\qquad t\longmapsto\pi\widetilde t.
\]

Here a tilde denotes any lift to `R`. This is well defined because
`m^2=0`, is surjective because `m=(pi)`, and is injective: if a unit
annihilated `pi`, then `pi=0`. A nonzero residue has only unit lifts.
Thus `ann(pi)=m`, `|m|=q`, and `|R|=q^2`. Lift choices disappear whenever
they are multiplied by `pi`; no ring embedding `F -> R` is assumed.

The projective directions have the disjoint normal forms

\[
 (1,a)\quad(a\in R),\qquad (\pi\widetilde t,1)\quad(t\in F).
                                                               \tag{4}
\]

Indeed normalize the first coordinate if it is a unit; otherwise normalize
the second. There are `q^2+q=q(q+1)` directions, exactly `q` above each
of the `q+1` directions of `F^2`. Every full line has `q^2` points.
Nonunimodular nonzero vectors are `pi` times unimodular vectors, so the
alternative convention requiring a line in every vector direction gives
the same Kakeya condition.

We use **Blokhuis--Mazzocca, Proposition 7**: any Kakeya set in the affine
plane over an odd-order field has at least

\[
                  M_q=\frac{q^2+2q-1}{2}              \tag{5}
\]

points. This theorem applies to supersets of chosen line unions as well.
Their proof is an external mathematical input, not supplied by the finite
checks in this package. See [SOURCES.md](SOURCES.md).

## 2. One residue-direction group

Translate a lift of the common residue point to zero. Fix a direction
`d` in `F^2`. An invertible linear map over `F` taking `d` to horizontal
lifts to one over `R` because its determinant lifts to a unit. We may
therefore count this group using coordinates in which its `q` lines are

\[
      y=\pi\widetilde t\,x+\pi\widetilde{b(t)},\qquad t\in F,     \tag{6}
\]

for an arbitrary function `b:F -> F`. They have intercepts in `m`
precisely because their reductions are the horizontal line through zero.

For `r in F`, points over the residue point `(r,0)` have the unique
form `x=tilde r+pi tilde X`, `y=pi tilde Y`, with `X,Y in F`.
Equation (6) is exactly

\[
                         Y=tr+b(t).                   \tag{7}
\]

In particular, its union occupies exactly

\[
                  q\,|\{tr+b(t):t\in F\}|             \tag{8}
\]

points in that residue fiber. This is an identity, including all mixed-
characteristic carry terms, because the discarded terms contain `pi^2`.

In an auxiliary affine plane `F^2`, take the `q` lines
`Y=tX+b(t)` and the vertical line `X=0`. This is a finite-field Kakeya
set. Its size is exactly

\[
               q+\sum_{r\in F^*}|\{tr+b(t):t\in F\}|.
\]

By (5), the sum is at least `(q^2-1)/2`. Thus the original group covers
at least

\[
                        \frac{q(q^2-1)}2              \tag{9}
\]

points above its `q-1` nonzero residue points. This is the reusable local
bridge: the excluded central fiber is replaced by one full transverse
line in a finite-field plane.

## 3. Assemble the fibers

Different directions through zero in `F^2` have disjoint nonzero point
sets. Therefore (9) can be added over all `q+1` residue directions
without overlap. Outside the central fiber, the chosen system covers
at least `(q+1)q(q^2-1)/2` points.

Identify the central fiber `m x m` with `F^2` using multiplication
by `pi` in both coordinates. Each original line meets it in exactly `q` points forming
an affine line of its residue direction. One can see this directly in
(6) at `r=0`, and then undo the coordinate change. Since all residue
directions occur, the central-fiber union is itself a finite-field
Kakeya set and has at least `M_q` points. Adding the disjoint pieces gives

\[
 |K|\ge\frac{(q+1)q(q^2-1)}2+M_q
      =\frac{q^4+q^3+q-1}2,
\]

which proves (1). The argument concerns an arbitrary witnessing system;
extra points of `K` can only increase its size.

## 4. An explicit smaller competitor

Let `S={a^2:a in R}`. Every nonunit squares to zero. A unit is a square
in `R` exactly when its residue is a nonzero square in `F`: lift a
nonzero square root, then solve the linear correction using the invertible
derivative `2a`. Each nonzero square residue has all `q` lifts as squares.
Consequently

\[
                       |S|=1+\frac{q(q-1)}2.          \tag{10}
\]

Take the union of the `q^2` quadratic-intercept lines

\[
 T=\bigcup_{a\in R}\{(x,ax-a^2):x\in R\}.
\]

Completing the square, valid because two is a unit, gives the exact
membership criterion

\[
                 (x,y)\in T\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
                           x^2-4y\in S.               \tag{11}
\]

For every fixed `x`, the map `y -> x^2-4y` is bijective. Hence
`|T|=q^2|S|`. These lines supply all directions `(1,a)` in (4).

Choose `y_0 in R` for which `-bar y_0` is a quadratic nonresidue in
`F`; such choices exist for every odd `q`. Add

\[
 V=\bigcup_{t\in F}\{(\pi\widetilde t(y-y_0),y):y\in R\},
 \qquad K_*=T\cup V.                                  \tag{12}
\]

These `q` lines have exactly the remaining directions in (4). Thus `K_*`
is Kakeya. Its size is computed by disjoint residue rows as follows.

For a fixed `y`, if `bar y != bar y_0`, the points of `V` have every
`x in m`; if `bar y=bar y_0`, they have only `x=0`. In all cases `x^2=0`,
so (11) becomes `-4y in S`.

- There are `(q-1)/2` nonzero residue classes with `-bar y` a
  nonsquare, including `bar y_0`. Each of the other `(q-3)/2` classes
  contributes `q^2` points to `V minus T`; the exceptional class
  contributes `q`.
- In the zero residue class there are `q` lifts of `y`. Only `y=0`
  has `-4y in S`, so this class contributes `q(q-1)` new points.
- The remaining nonzero classes contribute none: all their lifts have
  square discriminant by (10).

Therefore

\[
 |V\setminus T|=\frac{q-3}{2}q^2+q+q(q-1)
              =\frac{q^2(q-1)}2.
\]

Using (10),

\[
 |K_*|=q^2\left(1+\frac{q(q-1)}2\right)
                  +\frac{q^2(q-1)}2
        =\frac{q^4+q^2}2.
\]

This proves (2), and comparison yields the stated exclusion (3).

## Scope, attribution and validation

The finite-field lower bound, local-ring Kakeya framework, and quadratic
line-union method are established ideas. The contribution is their
residue-fiber assembly into a uniform obstruction for extremizers, with
an explicit quantitative separator. No claim of historical priority is
made beyond the bounded primary-source audit in [SOURCES.md](SOURCES.md).

Odd residue characteristic and `m^2=0` are hypotheses of this theorem.
It says nothing about minimum sets in higher ring lengths or arbitrary
finite rings. In particular it does not preclude measure-zero p-adic or
formal-power-series Kakeya sets with concurrent first reductions; such
infinite constructions are already known. Minimization at length two
is a different property. It also distinguishes smallest cardinality from
inclusion minimality: no statement excludes residue pencils from being
inclusion-minimal Kakeya sets.

[verify.py](verify.py) reconstructs actual ring lines, compares their point
sets with the discriminant and residue-fiber formulas, checks all direction
normalizations, and exhausts the smallest pencil systems. These are exact
finite corroborations of the written theorem. The universal result and
its imported finite-field theorem are not proved by extrapolating those
checks, and independent peer review or formalization is not included.
